01 Introduction & intended use

The Dual-Laser PDH Driver is a precision, two-channel controller for a pair of 1550 nm distributed-feedback (DFB) butterfly laser modules. Each channel provides a low-noise constant-current laser drive, a bipolar linear thermo-electric-cooler (TEC) temperature controller, RF frequency modulation, and a synchronous-demodulation lock loop that holds the laser onto the side of an optical resonance.

The instrument is used as a differential optical thermometer. Two independent lasers, A and B, are each locked to their own fringe of a shared reference ring/cavity. The quantity of interest is the beat frequency between the two lasers:

Δν = S · (TB − TA),   S ≈ 2 GHz/K

Because the measurement is ratiometric and material-defined, slow common-mode drift (of the cavity, the room, the electronics) cancels, and the figure of merit is the within-60 s Allan deviation expressed in equivalent kelvin. A kHz digital dither lock reaches ≈40 nK; the RF analog-derivative lock this hardware runs targets the ≈4–11 nK regime, limited primarily by the laser's residual amplitude modulation (RAM), not by the electronics.

Architecture. The instrument is built as a set of interchangeable 3U Eurocards on a passive backplane, so that channels, detection, and each power rail are separately serviceable and any card fits any slot. The remainder of this manual documents each card and the theory that ties them together.

02 Safety

Mains voltage. The AC Board and the chassis-mounted toroidal transformers carry 120 VAC line voltage. Only qualified personnel should open the enclosure with power applied. The IEC inlet fuse (2 A slow-blow) and the SL22 inrush thermistor are the only primary-side protection — do not defeat them.
Invisible laser radiation, 1550 nm. The optical output is invisible and can damage the retina. Never look into a fiber connector or the butterfly output. Cap unused fibers, and never energize a laser channel with an open, unterminated fiber pointing toward personnel. Follow your site's Class 3B/4 laser-safety procedures.
Laser reverse voltage. The DFB die tolerates only ≈2 V reverse. The per-channel Schottky clamp (BAT46W) is mandatory — never bypass it, and confirm laser polarity before first power-on.
Live heatsinks. On the pm22V and pm6V supplies the pass-transistor tabs (and therefore their heatsinks, unless isolated) sit at the raw rail potential — up to ≈±28 V — not ground. Keep the two tall sinks clear of each other and of the chassis, or isolate the devices and ground the sinks.
Power-on discipline. Firmware always parks both laser DACs at code 0 and both TEC channels at 0 A on reset and after any fault. Never command a non-zero laser current before the supply's power-good is asserted.

03 Theory of operation

This section walks the complete signal path — from set-point to locked laser — and derives the governing numbers for each stage, explaining why each block is built the way it is.

3.1 Why a differential lock at all

The physical measurand is a tiny temperature difference between two samples read out through the optical frequency of light. A single laser locked to a cavity inherits every drift of that cavity. Running two lasers through the same resonator and taking their beat note makes the result depend only on the differential path: cavity length drift, common thermal expansion, and shared electronic offsets appear identically on both arms and subtract out. What survives is S·(TB−TA) with S ≈ 2 GHz/K, so a 1 nK temperature difference is a ~2 Hz beat shift — comfortably countable against a 10 MHz reference over the 60 s window.

This is deliberately not textbook Pound–Drever–Hall. A true dispersive PDH lock on the Q ≈ 105, 1.93 GHz resonance would need ~GHz phase modulation, which neither laser-current modulation nor the on-board AD9833 DDS can reach. Instead the laser frequency is modulated at a chosen Ω just above the DFB's thermal→carrier FM crossover, and the loop demodulates the slope of the ring transmission — a high-frequency lock-in / derivative discriminator. The PDH name is retained for the family of techniques.

3.2 Laser channel — the constant-current (ACC) core

Each laser is driven by a low-side N-channel MOSFET current sink closed around a precision op-amp (OPA192). The DAC set-point enters the non-inverting input through a resistive scaler; the voltage across a 4-terminal Kelvin sense resistor feeds back to the inverting input. The loop forces the sense voltage to equal the scaled set-point regardless of the laser's forward voltage:

Vsense = Ilaser · Rsense  (Rsense = 0.25 Ω)
Vset = VDAC · Rb/(Ra+Rb) = VDAC · 909R/200.9k ≈ VDAC/221
Loop ⇒ Ilaser · 0.25 Ω = VDAC/221

At full scale the 5.000 V reference gives Vset = 5.0/221 = 22.6 mV, i.e. Ilaser = 22.6 mV / 0.25 Ω ≈ 90.5 mA. The set-point resolution follows directly from the 16-bit DAC:

Resolution = Vref / 216 / 221 / Rsense = 5.000 / 65536 / 221 / 0.25 ≈ 1.4 µA/LSB

Why this topology. The ÷221 scaler deliberately compresses the full 5 V DAC span into a 22.6 mV control window so that the DAC's own noise and the op-amp offset are divided down by 201 before they become current noise. A Kelvin (4-terminal) sense resistor keeps the milliohms of trace and solder out of the 0.25 Ω, and the BAT46W clamp protects the ≤2 V reverse-rated die. The PDH correction is summed into that same node through R96 7.5 MΩ, giving 0.483 mA/V of servo authority: an OPA192 railed to +14.8 V adds only +5.9 mA, so DAC full scale plus a railed integrator comes to 96.4 mA — inside the DFB1550LP absolute maximum of 100 mA. Because U24 sits at VREF_MID = 2.500 V when the error is zero, R134 15 MΩ to −5 V cancels that pedestal (an exact 2:1 with R96, so even R134's 5 % tolerance costs only ~63 nA).

How the set-point summing node is scaled. R67 is the scaler bottom and returns to ground, so the DAC weight and the servo weight are set independently rather than being forced to sum to 1. The PDH integrator injects through R96 7.5 MΩ, and R134 15 MΩ to −5 V cancels the integrator’s 2.500 V idle pedestal. The result is a DAC full scale of 90.5 mA and a servo authority of 0.483 mA/V, so even a railed integrator on top of DAC full scale reaches only 96.4 mA — inside the laser’s 100 mA absolute maximum.

3.3 TEC channel — bipolar linear H-bridge

Laser frequency is set by die temperature, so the TEC loop is what the lock ultimately steers on long time scales. It is a four-quadrant linear bridge: two conducting FETs pass current through the Peltier element in either direction, delivering +2.5 A of cooling to −1.5 A of heating. The two half-bridges are driven in antiphase: U21 buffers the error amplifier straight onto leg 2 (Q17/Q18), while U106 inverts it about ground and U107 buffers that onto leg 1 (Q13/Q14). Each leg is a complementary source follower — IRLB8721 N-channel on top, IRF9540N P-channel below with its source on the bridge node — and the 15 V gate clamps to the ±6 V rails bound each leg to about ±4 V. The 470 Ω gate resistors hold a saturated buffer to 35 mA; at the original 47 Ω a saturated loop demanded 353 mA from a 200 mA OPA551 and drove it into thermal shutdown. A switching (PWM) bridge was rejected because its ripple would couple into the shared package thermal mass and modulate the very temperature being controlled.

The unipolar 0–5 V DAC is referenced to a virtual ground so that a mid-scale code is not zero current — a buffered reference (TEC_VZERO, OPA192 U17) sets the zero point. TEC_VZERO is ≈1.20 V, from R41 3.16 kΩ / R42 1 kΩ off 5Vref (5.000 × 1/4.16 = 1.2019 V), trimmed by the setpoint DAC through R117/R43. It is not VREF_MID and not the centre of the span: zero current sits at 24% of the 0–5 V range, which is where the asymmetric +2.5 A cool / −1.5 A heat envelope comes from. Codes below it heat; codes above it cool. Firmware uses TEC_DAC_ZERO_CODE = 15728 (1.20/5.00 × 65535) and TEC_IMON_OFFSET_V = 1.20. The current transfer is set by the scaler around that zero and the 0.1 Ω Kelvin sense:

ITEC = (VDAC − VZERO) · (Rbottom/Rtop) / Rsense
Rsense = 0.1 Ω,  scaler ≈ 20 kΩ / 4.99 kΩ  ⇒  250 mV at sense = 2.5 A full-scale cool

How the loop closes. U32 (INA828, G = 1 + 50k/8.06k = 7.20, REF on TEC_VZERO) measures the 0.1 Ω shunt, and its output TEC_IMON = 1.202 + 0.72·I is the feedback — so this is a true current loop. R133 10 kΩ with C171 100 nF around U19 makes it a dominant-pole integrator at 159 Hz (loop crossover ~115 Hz), far below the gate pole and the INA828 bandwidth. Cooling current reads above 1.20 V, matching TEC_IMON_OFFSET_V in firmware; at zero current U19's output sits at 0 V, where both legs are equal.

VZERO trim range. The setpoint DAC trims TEC_VZERO into the VZERO_SET node through R117 in series with R43 20 kΩ. DAC_VZERO_TRIM carries no shunt capacitor, so R117 performs no filtering — it only adds to the injection resistance and shrinks the trim span:

Linear-bridge dissipation. The two conducting FETs drop the entire (Vrail − VTEC)·I. Worst case is a few watts per channel, which is why the dedicated ±6 V TEC supply is a heavy multi-amp linear rail and the FETs need ≤10 °C/W to chassis. Power-up safe state. A unipolar DAC needs a non-zero code for zero current, so no reset state of U101 can mean “TEC off” — RSTSEL only picks which wrong state you boot into. RSTSEL is tied to GND, which parks the laser DAC safely at 0 mA but would otherwise leave the TEC commanding −1.34 A of heating; tying it to VLOGIC instead would fix the TEC only by putting 45 mA of uncommanded current through the laser. Instead U108 (ADG419) forces the setpoint node to TEC_VZERO — exactly 0 A — whenever TEC_EN is low. TEC_EN is PCA9538 IO3 with a 10 kΩ pulldown, and the expander powers up with its IOs as high-Z inputs, so the bridge is off from the instant power is applied and stays off if the Teensy never boots. Firmware raises it only after parking the DAC at the zero-current code; tec1/tec2 off puts it back.

3.4 RF modulation & the derivative lock

The lock loop, per channel, is: modulate the laser current at Ω → the ring converts that FM into AM on the transmitted light → a wideband photodiode + TIA recovers it → an analog multiplier synchronously demodulates it against a phase-set reference at Ω → a low-pass leaves the DC error → an analog PI servo corrects the laser set-point. The Teensy supervises but no longer closes the fast loop.

RF injection — bias-tee, not gate coupling

The modulation is injected onto the laser line through a DC-block capacitor with a series choke isolating the DC loop, not onto the MOSFET gate. Gate injection would push the tone through the FET's nonlinear transconductance and stamp that nonlinearity onto the optical amplitude — manufacturing RAM, the one error the whole design is biased to minimize. The bias-tee keeps both the op-amp loop and the FET out of the RF amplitude path.

XL(Ω) = 2π·Ω·Lbt = 2π·2 MHz·10 µH ≈ 126 Ω ≫ (Rsense+rlaser) ✓ (RF flows into laser)
XC(Ω) = 1/(2π·Ω·Cbt) = 1/(2π·2 MHz·10 nF) ≈ 8 Ω ≪ Rinj ✓ (source couples in)
Ipk ≈ Vsrc,pk/Rinj = 0.6 V / 2 kΩ ≈ 0.30 mA pk

The 0.30 mA depth is chosen to give ≈0.1–0.3× the fringe half-width — enough discriminator slope, little enough RAM. Rinj is a trim so depth can be optimized once the laser's FM response is measured.

Wideband TIA

The transmission photocurrent is recovered by a wideband transimpedance amplifier (OPA814, running on ±5 V with its non-inverting input on ground) with bandwidth ≥ 5–10× Ω so the demodulated slope isn't rolled off. The feedback pair trades transimpedance against stability:

f−3dB ≈ √( GBWP / (2π·Rf·CTOT) ),   Cf(flat) ≈ √( CTOT / (2π·Rf·GBWP) )
As built (AFE card): Rf = 8.06 kΩ, Cf = 1 pF, referenced to GND — output 0 V dark, −8.06 mV/µA

CTOT is the whole capacitance seen at the summing node, not just the diode. With the FGA01FC capacitance now measured (Thorlabs product raw data, SpecSheets/fga01_cap(Capacitance).csv) the budget is closed:

Cd 2.13 pF (FGA01, measured at the 12 V bias) + Ccm 2.5 pF + Cdiff 0.2 pF (OPA814) + Cpcb 0.67 pF (extracted from the routing)  =  CTOT = 5.50 pF
GBWP 250 MHz ⇒ Cf(flat) = 0.66 pF, Butterworth f−3dB = 30 MHz  |  as fitted Cf = 1 pF ⇒ f−3dB = 1/(2π·Rf·Cf) = 19.7 MHz
The TIA is deliberately over-compensated — leave Cf at 1 pF. Cf = 1 pF was originally sized for CTOT ≈ 12.7 pF; the real budget is 5.50 pF, so the stage sits about 1.5× past flat. That is the safe direction: over-damped, no gain peaking, and 19.7 MHz is still 7.9× the 2.5 MHz demodulation tone — inside the ≥ 5–10× rule above.

Synchronous demodulation

An AD835 four-quadrant analog multiplier (U45/U46) forms the phase detector, multiplying the TIA output by a phase-set reference. At the modest 2–2.5 MHz demodulation frequency a true linear multiplier is the low-noise choice: unlike a switching mixer it injects no synchronous charge-injection offset, and because it is driven by a clean sine — not a hard-switched square — it folds no odd-harmonic noise onto the baseband error. (This replaces the earlier AD831 RF mixer, whose 500 MHz bandwidth was 200× more than a 2 MHz demodulator needs and which, like any hard-switched mixer, would have folded harmonics.) That reference is a dedicated per-channel AD9833 — four DDS total (two drive, two reference) all sharing one 25 MHz clock so every tone is phase-coherent, and the two channels stay independent (ΩA ≠ ΩB). Because the DDS sine is only ~0.6 Vpp and the multiplier's discriminator slope scales with LO amplitude, each reference is first boosted by an OPA820 ×3 buffer to nearly fill the AD835's ±1 V input range. The multiplier output contains a DC term proportional to the fringe slope error plus a 2Ω product; the 2-pole low-pass (fc ~50 kHz) keeps the error and rejects the 2Ω term. That baseband error then drives the analog PI loop filter below, whose output sums into the laser set-point to close the lock.

3.5 Reference & digitization chain

Every set-point traces back to one buried-Zener reference, an LTC6655-5 (5.000 V, 0.25 ppm), RC-filtered to ~16 Hz before it reaches the DACs, so reference noise is ≈1.6 µV p-p. Each signal card digitizes locally over I²C — a 16-bit DAC (AD5696R/MCP4728 class) makes the set-points and an ADS1115 reads the monitors — rather than busing dozens of raw analog lines across the backplane. The AFE carries a DAC of its own for the same reason: rather than a trimpot an operator has to revisit, its ERR offset is nulled by firmware and stored (§15.8). Only two analog signals per channel are bused: the demodulated error (ERRn, driven by the AFE) and the modulation drive (MODn, driven by the DIG DDS).

Noise rule. I²C is static when idle, so firmware runs a "quiet mode" during lock (telemetry poll ≤10 Hz, suspended entirely during lock acquisition), pull-ups live only on the DIG card, and each card entry has a 100 Ω series + local RC. Modulation frequencies are kept away from N×100/400 kHz so switching artifacts don't fold through the PDH demodulator.

3.6 Power architecture — and why it is all linear

A frequency-stabilized laser is exquisitely sensitive to supply noise, so every rail is linear; there is not a single switching converter in the instrument. The chain is:

120 VAC ─▶ AC Board ─┬─ T1 (2×18V) ─▶ ±18Vac ─▶ pm22V ─▶ ±18V bus ─▶ pm1p8V ─▶ ±15V, ±5V, 5Vref, 3V3, 1V8, +5V_LASER └─ T2 (2× 9V) ─▶ ± 9Vac ─▶ pm6V ─▶ ±6V_TEC (multi-amp) ─▶ CH-card TEC bridges

The two DC power cards use two different linear philosophies for good reason:

  • pm22V is a capacitance multiplier, not a regulator. The downstream pm1p8V card re-regulates everything to precise rails, so pm22V only has to deliver a quiet, low-dropout ±18 V. A source-follower MOSFET whose gate is filtered by a slow RC passes the raw rail with its ripple stripped off — cheap, cool, and low-drop, exactly what a pre-regulator wants to be.
  • pm6V is a true regulated supply, because the TEC load is high-current and its rail sets bridge dissipation directly. It is a discrete Vbe-tracking linear regulator with paralleled TO-264 Darlington pass transistors — regulation good enough to keep the TEC rails stable to tens of millivolts over temperature, current capacity (parallel devices + emitter ballasting) to source multiple amps continuously.

The ±15 V analog rails are made by a TPS7A39 dual tracking LDO, chosen because a single shared internal reference makes the + and − outputs track ratiometrically at every instant — a mathematical consequence of the topology, not a trimmed match:

VOUTP = +Vref·(1 + R1P/R2P),  VOUTN = −Vref·(1 + R1N/R2N)
Equal ramp at startup ⇒ rails always equal-and-opposite (no differential thermal transient into the DFB).

The individual rails and their derivations are detailed per-card in §09. The full rail-by-rail current budget lives in the companion separate power-review document.

04 Optical measurement setup

This section describes what sits outside the crate. The instrument itself never sees the measurand: it holds two lasers on two resonances, and the quantity you actually record — the optical beat between them — is formed in fiber and counted by external equipment. Understanding where the boundary falls matters, because the terms that dominate the error budget in §18 live out here, in optics the instrument cannot correct for.

4.1 The optical chain end to end

The chain divides into three parts with quite different characters. The drive path (solid, outbound) is the instrument imposing a frequency on each laser. The feedback path (dashed) is what makes the lock possible — without the taps there is no error signal and the servo has nothing to act on. The readout path (below the mixer) is pure measurement: it is deliberately open-loop, and nothing on it feeds back into the crate.

4.2 Why the measurement is a beat and not two frequencies

Neither laser's absolute frequency is known to anything like the precision the result implies — a free-running DFB wanders by many megahertz, and the instrument contains no absolute frequency reference. What is precise is the difference. Both lasers are locked to rings on the same die, so any effect common to both — die temperature drifting as a whole, mount strain, ambient pressure — moves both resonances together and cancels in the subtraction:

Δν = νB − νA = S·(TB − TA),   S = dν/dT ≈ 2 GHz/K

Two consequences follow, and they shape the whole design. First, absolute accuracy does not matter: the scale factor S is a property of the silicon, not of anything that needs calibrating, so the instrument never needs an absolute reference or a traceable frequency standard. Second, the figure of merit is within-window stability, not accuracy — how quietly the beat sits over a ~60 s window. That is why §18 is built around an Allan deviation at τ ≈ 60 s rather than a conventional accuracy budget, and why terms that drift slowly relative to that window are listed as cancelling.

Detecting the two lasers on one photodiode is what turns an optical-frequency difference into something countable. At 1550 nm each optical frequency is ≈193 THz — utterly uncountable — but their difference lands in the RF, where an ordinary counter works. With S ≈ 2 GHz/K, a 1 nK temperature difference is ≈2 Hz of beat, so nanokelvin resolution becomes a routine RF frequency measurement rather than an optical metrology problem.

4.3 Fiber connectors — APC on the laser side, PC at the detector

The connector types are not interchangeable, and the reason is worth stating plainly because mismatching them is an easy and expensive mistake.

  • Laser outputs use FC/APC (angled physical contact, 8° polish). The angle sends any reflection at the fiber end face out into the cladding instead of straight back down the core. A DFB laser is acutely sensitive to back-reflection: light returning into the cavity competes with the intended lasing mode and produces mode hops, linewidth broadening, and excess intensity noise — all of which appear directly in the lock as error-signal noise or as a lost lock. APC is cheap insurance for the one interface where reflection actually hurts.
  • The beat detector terminates in FC/PC (flat polish). There is no laser cavity downstream to protect — a photodiode does not care about reflections — so the simpler, lower-loss, more common connector is the right choice.
Never mate an APC connector to a PC connector. The 8° angle leaves an air wedge between the ferrules: you lose several decibels, the reflection you were trying to avoid comes back anyway, and forcing the mating can permanently damage both end faces. APC ferrules are keyed and conventionally green; PC are blue or black. Keep the two families physically separate, and if a channel shows unexplained loss or a suddenly noisy lock, check the connector types before suspecting the electronics.

4.4 Optical power budget

The photodetector converts optical power to current linearly:

Ipd = ℛ · Popt,   ℛ ≈ 1 A/W

The binding constraint is not sensitivity but survival. The beat detector has a damage threshold of Pmax ≈ 18 mW, and because the mixer combines both through arms onto that single detector, it sees the sum of what the two rings deliver. That ceiling propagates backwards through the whole optical layout: it caps the usable power out of the microrings, which together with the splitter ratio caps how hard the lasers may be driven.

The split ratio is therefore a genuine design trade rather than a detail. Sending more light to the taps gives the AFE transimpedance amplifiers a larger photocurrent and a better signal-to-noise ratio on the error signal — but leaves less for the beat. Sending more to the through arms improves beat contrast but starves the lock. Set the ratio so the lock has comfortable margin first: a noisy beat on a solid lock is still a measurement, whereas a lock that drops out produces nothing at all.

Check the power before connecting the detector. Measure at the mixer output with a power meter and confirm it is below 18 mW with both channels running at full drive — not just at the operating point. A sweep during acquisition (§15) can transiently put a ring on resonance at maximum laser current, which is exactly when the through arm is brightest. Add a fixed attenuator if the margin is thin; the detector is easier to protect than to replace.

4.5 Keeping the two channels from talking to each other

Both lasers are amplitude-modulated for their derivative locks (§3.4), and both end up on the same photodiode. If the two channels used the same modulation frequency, their modulation sidebands would beat against each other and land as a spurious tone right in the band where the real measurement lives — a systematic error that would look like a temperature difference.

The fix is to run the two channels at deliberately different modulation frequencies, set per channel with omega1 / omega2. Each AFE demodulator is then sensitive only to its own channel's tone, and the crosstalk product falls outside the differential beat's band of interest. Choose the two frequencies so that neither their difference nor low-order harmonics land near the beat frequency you expect to measure.

4.6 What good looks like

With the optics assembled and both channels locked, expect the following. Both channels report LOCKED with a small, stationary error signal (§15). The beat on the counter is a single clean tone, not a wandering or multi-valued reading — a jumpy count usually means one laser is mode-hopping rather than that the temperature is moving. Breathing on the die should move the beat by megahertz and it should settle back, which confirms end-to-end sensitivity and correct sign. Blocking one tap should drop that channel out of lock while the other holds, confirming the two feedback paths are independent and not swapped.

Share one timebase. Where possible, drive the frequency counter from the same 10 MHz house reference the DIG card uses. The counter's timebase enters the result directly, and the DIG card already brings a buffered 10 MHz out to backplane pin c20 (§11). A shared reference removes the counter's own drift from the measurement; with a common timebase the contribution is negligible, which is why §18 lists it near zero.

5.2 Card addressing — one jumper, on the CH cards only

Every card sets its own identity on-card, never from the slot. An earlier plan reserved three connector pins (a20/c18/c19) for geographic addressing; on every board they were wired to nothing but the connector itself, so v7 removes them from the connector entirely.

The CH cards are the only boards with an address jumper, and in v7 they carry exactly one. GA0 has a 10 kΩ pull-up to 3V3 and a two-pin jumper (JP101) to ground: fitted = 0, open = 1. That single bit sets the card's I²C addresses and steers the on-card ADG419 muxes that pick which bused analog pair (ERR1/MOD1 vs ERR2/MOD2) the card uses — so a CH card is wholly "channel 1" or wholly "channel 2" with nothing to set inconsistently. Every other card is hard-strapped with no jumper at all. A card can therefore sit in any slot — identity travels with the card, not the slot.

Other cards. The DIG card is the bus master and needs no address. The AFE DAC is not interchangeable with the CH DACs. All three are AD5696Rs, but the CH cards tie the GAIN pin to VLOGIC (gain 2, 0–5 V span) while the AFE ties it to GND (gain 1, 0–2.5 V span). The same code word therefore means a different output voltage on the two cards.

5.3 Fault handling

FAULT_n (b20) is an open-drain wired-OR line any card can pull low. On assertion the Teensy edge-detects it, then polls each card over I²C to find the source, and parks lasers and TECs safe. Sources include the CH-card H-bridge Flag pins and the DIG-card voltage monitor (any rail out of its window pulls the line via the D303–D311 diode-OR). LOCK_EN and per-card status live in I²C registers (PCA9538 GPIO expanders) rather than dedicated bus wires.

07 pm22V — ±18 V supply

Laser_Driver_v7/pm22V. A 160×100 mm 3U Eurocard dual-rail supply. Despite the folder name it is targeted at ±18–20 V; it is a capacitance multiplier / low-dropout filter, not a regulator. Its job is to hand the pm1p8V card a quiet ±18 V from which precise rails are made.

Topology (per rail)

±18Vac ─▶ D1 bridge (GBPC1510) ─▶ reservoir C3/C4/C9/C10 (2200µF) ─▶ FET source-follower ─▶ R8/R9 (0.47Ω) ─▶ ±18V @ J6
+rail FET = Q1 IRF540N (NMOS),  −rail FET = Q2 IRF9540N (PMOS)

Each pass FET is wired as a source follower whose gate is held at the raw rail through a 100 kΩ resistor and bled to the source through 1 MΩ, with a 470 nF gate capacitor. That RC gives a ~47 ms soft-start and, in steady state, presents the FET gate with a heavily filtered version of the raw rail; the source (output) then follows that clean gate voltage, multiplying the effective reservoir capacitance by the FET's gain. A 12 V Zener clamps VGS.

Output ≈ raw rail − VGS(drop) ≈ ±18–20 V loaded (≈±28 V unloaded before the load pulls it down)
Why not a regulator here. A cap-multiplier drops far less voltage than an LDO and dissipates far less, so from an ~±21 V rectified rail it comfortably delivers the ≥±18 V the ±15 V regulators downstream need for headroom — while cleaning ripple. Precise regulation is deferred to pm1p8V.

Reservoir placement (design note)

The bulk reservoir belongs on the rectifier/drain side (C9/C10 across each raw rail to ground), not only on the FET output: with a smoothed drain the gate RC filters it and the FET follows clean. Without a drain-side reservoir the bridge feeds an unsmoothed full-wave waveform into the drain and the cap-multiplier degenerates into an ordinary lossy reservoir supply — still DC, but noisier and with high FET peak currents.

Thermal & connectors

  • Heatsinks: Q1/Q2 on Boyd 531102B02500G (TO-220); bridge D1 on a Boyd 6223BG bolt-on sink.
  • Input: 3-position Phoenix screw terminal (±18Vac + CT). Output: DIN 41612 Type C 96-way (J6) — the ±18 V rail columns to the backplane.
Center tap is mandatory. This is a single-bridge + CT-ground split supply; the transformer center tap defines board ground. If the CT is left floating the rails go wildly asymmetric. (During bench bring-up a bridge-footprint pin mismatch shorted the secondary — buzz, heat, collapsed rails — and a floating CT gave +36/−23 V; both are documented failure modes, now fixed on the board.)

The regulation is a classic discrete Vbe-tracking loop. VKA = 1.225 · (1 + Rtop/Rbottom) = 1.225 · (1 + 60.4k/15k) = 6.16 V
Rail output = base ∓ 2·Vbe ≈ ±6.0 V

Why two pass devices per rail. A single transistor can't carry multiple amps continuously within a Eurocard's thermal envelope, so each rail parallels two TO-264 devices, each with a 0.1 Ω emitter-ballast resistor to force current sharing. Laser_Driver_v7/Backplane. A passive 2-layer 8-slot backplane carrying 8× DIN 41612 Type C 96-way female connectors (J1–J8) on an exact 50.8 mm (10 HP) pitch, sized to drop into the 84 HP subrack with 4 HP to spare.

Function & interaction

  • Rail distribution. Every rail buses across all eight slots as one island. Ampacity comes from ganging contacts rather than from heroic copper: each rail owns whole a/b/c columns of the Type C connector, so the heavy TEC rails present six contacts per slot (+6V_TEC on columns 1–2, PGND_TEC on 3–4, −6V_TEC on 5–6) and the pinout is designed at a derated 1.5 A per contact.

The system runs two separate ground nets: GND (analog / signal / logic return, shared by every card and the ±18 V, ±5 V and logic rails) as one plane, and PGND_TEC (the ±6 V TEC-supply return) as a second, deliberately separate plane. That tie is mandatory: without it the isolated TEC domain floats, and the ±15 V-referenced TEC current-sense (INA828) and H-bridge control op-amps on the CH card would see an undefined input common-mode and mis-read or clip.
  • Signals. I²C (SDA_BUS/SCL_BUS), the two bused analog pairs (ERR1/2_BUS, MOD1/2_BUS), 5Vref, VREF_MID, the wired-OR FAULT_n and the spare CLK10M_BUS run to every slot. Three further columns per side are left as labelled spares.
Single-point ground bond — fit at one place only. The bond carries only op-amp bias / leakage current: the TEC load current loops entirely within the ±6 V / PGND_TEC domain and never returns through it, which is why the reference is a low-impedance link rather than a resistor.
Placement. Put the pm6V (TEC source) slot adjacent to the CH consumer slots to keep the TEC-rail IR drop under ~0.1 V. Decoupling lives on the cards (HF bypass at each IC); the only backplane cap worth adding is a bulk electrolytic at the TEC-rail entry to stiffen the shared bus.

Function

  • 4× AD9833 DDS (25 MHz clock): U34/U39 generate the two modulation drives (MOD1/MOD2_DRV), U35/U38 the two phase-set demod references.   • Front-panel LO outputs — the two phase-set reference DDS channels leave the card on their own SMAs: U35 → R312 49.9 Ω → J302 (LO1_OUT) and U38 → R313 49.9 Ω → J302B1 (LO2_OUT). Interaction

The DIG card is the I²C master and DDS source. Freed monitor/enable pins from the old point-to-point design migrate to per-card I²C GPIO.

Mating the BTF stack. The TEC pair (butterfly pins 1 and 14) carries up to 2.5 A across the board-to-board connector; the laser (pins 10/11) is ≤100 mA and the rest is µA–mA. The Samtec pins are rated well above this, so current is not the concern — seating is. Fit all four M3 standoffs before mating, press the stack down square, and confirm it is fully home: the specified insertion window is only 2.67 mm wide, and a partially mated connector can open the TEC circuit in service, stranding the laser at temperature with no control loop. Do not use the header alone to hold the card. Full stack detail is in §13.
TEC H-bridge heatsinks (HS1–HS4). The linear bridge burns the difference between the ±6 V rails and the Peltier voltage — up to ≈10 W in a single FET at full 2.5 A cooling, and any of the four can be the hot one depending on drive polarity. Each FET (Q13/Q14/Q17/Q18) mounts on its own Wakefield 657-10ABPE vertical heatsink. Every tab is live — Q13/Q17 at +6V_TEC, Q14/Q18 at the swinging bridge outputs — so isolate all four with the insulator + shoulder-washer kits (no bare tab-to-sink contact). Steady-state lock current is well under 1 A (≈3 W, cool); the firmware current limit and the OPA551 thermal flag bound the fault cases. Monitor-PD optical power. The DFB package's internal photodiode is biased from the NTC reference node, so its transimpedance output MPD_MON sits below NTC_A by an amount proportional to photocurrent. The firmware strips that offset — optical power ∝ (NTC_A − MPD_MON) — and reports it as POPTn in the d dump (scale set for the ≈2.2 µA/mW monitor responsivity; trim against a power meter at bring-up).
Optional configuration change — TEC integrator C171/R133. U19 is a pure integrator (no feedback resistor): R133 10 kΩ 0.1 % from TEC_IMON into the inverting input, C171 100 nF from that node to the output. Together they set the TEC servo integrator time constant, τ = R133 · C171 = 1 ms (unity gain at ≈159 Hz).

C171 is an X7R part as built. X7R is specified to ±15 % over −55…+125 °C, so the integrator time constant — and with it the TEC loop gain — can move by up to that much with ambient temperature. This is accepted in the current design: the TEC loop is thermally dominated with a time constant of seconds against a 159 Hz integrator corner, and ±15 % loop gain is ordinary design margin. The dielectric's voltage coefficient is a much smaller effect here, because 100 nF in an 0805 body at 50 V is a low-density part.

If the TEC loop is found to change behaviour with ambient temperature — hunting, overshoot or sluggish settling that tracks the room rather than the set-point — the integrator can be moved to a C0G dielectric with no board change, by trading capacitance for resistance so that τ is preserved:

13 BTF card — butterfly laser daughter card

Laser_Driver_v7/BTF_Card. A small mezzanine board that carries the DFB butterfly laser and stacks directly above its CH card. It replaces the flying 14-way harness of the earlier revisions: instead of a cable running from the card to an off-board laser mount, the laser now sits on its own PCB about 40 mm above the CH card, connected by a single board-to-board stack.

Why the laser moved onto a card

A butterfly package cannot mount on the CH card itself — its TEC dumps up to ≈2 A of heat and needs a substantial spreader, which will not fit among the precision analog stages. The earlier answer was an off-board mount plus a 24 AWG harness, but that puts a hand-built cable carrying 2.5 A of TEC current and the laser's own bias in series with everything, with a reversed-connector failure mode that destroys the diode. Stacking the laser on a daughter card removes the cable entirely: the connection becomes a keyed board-to-board mate, the pin order is fixed in copper, and the current path is short and paralleled across header pins.

On-card contents

  • ZIF socket pair — JZ1 (Azimuth 5253-100-7S) and JZ2 (5253-100-7R) take the butterfly's two 7-lead rows. The laser drops in; it is never soldered, so a failed or upgraded diode is swapped without touching the board.
  • LD1 — the laser itself, present in the design only as a mechanical placement outline (DFB1550LP_Butterfly14_Mech). It has no pads, which is why the board reports 14 schematic-parity items; that is expected, not a fault (see the note below).
  • J1 — ESQ-107-12-G-D 2×7 socket, the stack connection down to the CH card.
  • JP1 — laser shorting shunt across LD_A/LD_K. Fitted, it clamps the diode against handling ESD. It must be removed before the laser is driven.
  • H1–H4 — four M3 mounting holes for the standoffs that actually carry the stack.

Thermal path — the aluminium spreader and its heat sinks

A TEC does not remove heat, it moves it. Everything the cooler pulls out of the laser die, plus every watt the cooler itself burns doing so, arrives at the butterfly’s baseplate and has to leave the instrument from there. That baseplate is also CASE, pin 13 — the same node the connector’s shield band and laser guards return to — so the thermal path and the shield path are physically the same piece of metal.

The laser is therefore clamped to an aluminium heat spreader, and the spreader carries three heat sinks. The spreader’s job is to take the heat out of the butterfly’s small baseplate and spread it across a face wide enough to feed all three; the sinks’ job is to hand it to the air. Splitting the duty three ways is what makes the numbers work — a single sink of this family cannot reject the worst-case load at any airflow the crate can reasonably produce.

Phase-change material is a one-way operation. The T766 pad ships dry and is meant to be brought up past its transition temperature once, under clamping pressure, after which it wets the joint. Torque the M3 screws evenly, run the assembly warm once before trusting the thermal numbers, and do not lift and re-seat a sink casually — a disturbed pad does not recover its interface resistance.

How the assembly is carried

The BTF card holds the spreader — the block is not anchored to the subrack, so the entire laser-plus-spreader-plus-sinks assembly is supported by the daughter card, which is in turn supported by its four M3 standoffs down to the CH card, which finally lands in the crate’s card guides and DIN connector. That is a long load path for a solid lump of aluminium, and it is worth being deliberate about.

The spreader alone is about 59 cm³ of aluminium, roughly 160 g, and three ATS-1139s add perhaps another 90 g, so call the hanging mass ≈250 g. It is also physically larger than the board carrying it: at 134 mm the spreader is longer than the card’s 75 mm, so part of it is cantilevered past the PCB edge and the mass does not sit over the standoffs. Static loading is not the issue — a quarter of a kilogram is nothing for four M3 posts. The issue is moment: during insertion, extraction and transport the overhang turns into a lever on the four mounting holes and on the stack connector’s 2.67 mm insertion window.

Handle the card by the CH board, never by the spreader. With the assembly cantilevered off a 54 × 75 mm daughter board, using the aluminium as a grip applies the full moment straight to H1–H4 and to the ZIF sockets holding an irreplaceable laser. Support the spreader with the other hand whenever the pair is out of the crate, insert and extract slowly and squarely, and re-check the four M3 screws and the standoffs after any transport. If the crate is ever going to be shipped or moved as a unit, this overhang is the first thing to brace.

Fan plan

The intent is two fans, one per thermal domain: one over the power cards, whose linear pass elements are the largest single heat source in the crate, and one over the laser cards. The split is the right one — it matches the left/right layout of the crate (§5), and it keeps the air that has already passed over several tens of watts of pass transistors away from the two packages whose temperature is the measurement.

The laser-bay fan is not a demanding part. Pushing 2.0 m/s through the fin fields of three ATS-1139s needs only about 8–9 CFM of actual through-flow (roughly 2000 mm² of frontal area at 11.4 mm fin height). Unducted, most of a fan’s output bypasses the sinks, so specify perhaps three times that in free-air rating — a 25–30 CFM 80 mm fan is comfortable, and even a 60 mm part will do it. The power bay is the harder problem and should be sized against its own dissipation, not this one.

Two things to get right when the fans go in. First, the three sinks are only worth a third of one if they sit in parallel airflow; in line, one behind another, the rear sinks breathe pre-heated air and the assembly is meaningfully worse than 0.83 °C/W. Second, aim the air at the fin fields, not the package. At S ≈ 2 GHz/K, turbulent air playing directly over the butterfly body is a frequency disturbance the TEC loop has to reject, and fan vibration reaching the laser or its pigtail is a noise source the lock cannot reject at all. Duct or baffle the flow across the spreader, and mount the fan on grommets rather than hard to the subrack.

14 AFE card — analog detection front end

Laser_Driver_v7/AFE_Card. The shared detection card for both channels: it turns the two ring-transmission photocurrents into two clean, demodulated lock errors.

On-card blocks

  • Photodiode bias — an LT3042 ultra-low-noise +12.0 V bias (U1, R2 120 kΩ × 100 µA SET) reaching each cathode through R_S1/R_S2 100 Ω with C_K1/C_K2 100 nF to GND. The two fiber-coupled InGaAs photodiodes PD1/PD2 (FGA01FC) mount directly on the AFE card (Optical:PD_FGA01FC_EdgeBracket) — the pigtails are optical fiber, so nothing electrical is carried off-board. Anode → TIA summing node, cathode → biased and decoupled, case → GND. That placement is deliberate and load-bearing: at 100 nF the cathode is 0.08 Ω at 20 MHz, a hard AC ground, so the diode capacitance appears at the summing node exactly as the compensation model assumes. Running the detectors on wire instead would add ≈ 100 pF/m — 30 cm alone would swamp the 5.50 pF budget of §3.4 and leave Cf = 1 pF badly under-compensated.
  • Photodiode mounting — PCB-edge bracket. Each FGA01FC is held by a machined bracket that the can plugs into (Ø8.2 × 7.5 mm bore) and whose far end clamps the board's front edge in a 1.86 mm slot with two M2 cross screws, 13.6 mm apart and 2.5 mm in from the edge. The diode's own flange bolts to the bracket's front face with two M2 on the datasheet's 13.4 mm centres, so the board carries the detector and the front panel needs only a clearance hole — no over-constraint between a panel-held can and a board-held lead, which is what would otherwise fatigue the 11 mm leads on every card insertion. The can's back face sits 1.0 mm forward of the board edge. Pins 1 (K) and 3 (A) exit in the plane of the top copper and lie flat on surface pads — no bend at all; pin 2 (CASE) exits 1.25 mm lower, passes under the board and solders to a B.Cu pad. Clocking pin 2 downward is what makes the flange orientation unique: the flange mounts two ways, and the other one swaps cathode and anode. The two clamp holes are plated and carried on the CASE net, with 4.0 × 5.0 mm mask-free lands top and bottom, so the conductive bracket is bonded to GND at the point it shields — specify ENIG, a HASL bump makes a poor contact face. Screws: M2×6 or ×8 for the flange (the taps run the full 7.5 mm and open onto the face the board butts against), M2×8 for the clamp.
  • Wideband TIAs — OPA814 transimpedance amps (U30/U31), Rf 8.06 kΩ / Cf 1 pF, giving f−3dB = 19.7 MHz on a closed CTOT budget of = 5.50 pF (§3.4 — over-damped by design; do not "optimise" Cf downward). These run on ±5 V with their non-inverting inputs tied directly to GND — a true bipolar transimpedance stage, not the single-supply arrangement of the earlier revision. The summing node therefore sits at a real 0 V, and the output rests at 0 V in the dark and swings negative with light at −8.06 mV per µA of photocurrent (§3.4). Removing the mid-supply reference removes an entire error source: there is no longer a divider whose noise, drift and per-channel mismatch land directly on the summing node.
  • LO buffers — OPA820 ×3 non-inverting buffers (U204/U205) lift each ~0.6 Vpp DDS reference toward the AD835's ±1 V full scale, maximizing the discriminator slope.
  • Demodulators — AD835 four-quadrant analog multipliers (U45/U46) demodulating each TIA output against the buffered phase-set reference, followed by the 2-pole post-mix RC filters. Chosen over an RF mixer for lowest noise at the 2–2.5 MHz demod frequency — no switching offset, no harmonic folding (§3.4).
  • Error output stage — OPA192 (U202/U203) in an inverting configuration referenced to the VB node rather than a unity follower, so the demodulated error is both amplified and level-shifted into the positive-only window the DIG card's Teensy ADC can read. The input network is R116 3.3 kΩ in series with R118 6.65 kΩ (their junction shunted by C96 1 nF), so the DC gain is −R138/(R116+R118) = −49.9 k / 9.95 k = −5.02, with the non-inverting path seeing 1 + 49.9/9.95 = 6.02. A 47 Ω series resistor (R217/R218) then drives ERR1/ERR2 onto backplane c13/c14. See the note below on why R118 is 6.65 kΩ and not 10 kΩ.
  • Automatic ERR offset null — an AD5696R quad 16-bit I²C DAC (U206, address 0x0E) injects into the two VB nodes through 100 kΩ resistors, trimming out the demodulator pedestal under firmware control. This replaces the manual RV201/RV202 trimpots of the earlier revision (§15.8).
  • PD level monitor — because the TIA output is now bipolar, the level tap re-centres it before digitizing: R213/R214 10 kΩ from each TIA output and R134/R135 10 kΩ up to 5Vref form a two-resistor average, so PDn_LVL = (TIAOUT + 5.000 V)/2 — 2.500 V in the dark, falling at −4.03 mV/µA. The ADS1115 (0x4A) reads each one differentially against VREF_MID (AIN0–AIN1 and AIN2–AIN3), so a dark channel reads zero and increasing light reads increasingly negative, comfortably inside the ±4.096 V PGA range.
  • Front panel — dual isolated right-angle SMA for the LO / reference inputs.

Why Ri is split 3.3 k + 6.65 k — do not "round" R118 up to 10 kΩ. The VB node is VREF_MID (2.500 V) through R140 49.9 kΩ, down to GND through R141 8.25 kΩ, with the DAC injecting through R146 100 kΩ. That puts VB1 between 0.331 V and 0.497 V as the DAC sweeps its full 0–2.5 V span, and the stage's non-inverting gain of 6.02 turns that into a zero-error output of 1.99 V to 2.99 V — nominally 2.49 V with the DAC at its 1.25 V midscale, i.e. centred on VREF_MID. That centring is the whole point: the CH-card PI integrator compares ERRn against VREF_MID, so both cards must agree on where zero is or the servo locks to a displaced point.

The trap. R118/R119 look like the input resistor, so the obvious value is 10 kΩ against the 49.9 kΩ feedback. But R116/R117 (3.3 kΩ, the first post-mix filter pole) is in series ahead of them in the DC path — C96/C97 shunt the R116/R118 junction to ground, which is an AC pole, not a DC break — so R118 = 10 kΩ would make the true Ri 13.3 kΩ. The gain would fall to −3.75, the null range would collapse to 1.57–2.36 V, and 2.500 V would be unreachable: null1/null2 would drive the DAC to full scale and still fall short. 6.65 kΩ (E96) makes R116 + R118 = 9.95 kΩ, restoring ×5.02 and costing only a shift of the C96 pole from 64 kHz to 72 kHz.

Recentring instead by raising R141/R143 to 11.3 kΩ would fix the datum but leave the gain at ×3.75, so the single-resistor change is the correct one. If you respin the BOM, keep R118/R119 at 6.65 kΩ and R141/R143 at 8.25 kΩ.

Interaction

The AFE receives the phase-set reference tones from the DIG DDS, demodulates the two photodiode signals, and drives the bused ERRn lines that each CH card's PI servo consumes. Centralizing detection keeps the sensitive TIAs on one well-shielded card and lets the two CH cards stay identical.

15.4 Protection

  • TEC software current limit. Inside the 1 kHz loop each channel reads its TEC IMON, converts to amps via the INA828 scaling, and smoothly backs off the TEC DAC when current exceeds 2.5 A: a per-channel factor drops toward 0 (reaching zero in ~20 ms) and recovers in ~200 ms. It acts symmetrically on heating and cooling and never touches the laser loop.
  • H-bridge hardware fault. The OPA551 Flag pin is a current source (<50 nA healthy, 80 µA minimum in thermal shutdown), not an open-drain output. It drives R49 33 kΩ to ground with D23 clamping to 3V3, so the PCA9538 input reads active-HIGH: ~3 mV healthy, >2.6 V on shutdown. Both buffers' flags are wire-ORed — current sources simply sum. Before rev D the node was pulled up to 3V3 and read HIGH in both states, so a fault could never be detected. On assertion the servo-health bar is replaced by a red !! H-BRIDGE FAULT !! banner, the event prints to serial, and the JSON reports "hbf":1. On the backplane these OR into the shared FAULT_n line so the supervisor parks lasers and TECs safe.

15.7 Backplane integration & addressing

On the card-cage backplane the controller is also the I²C bus master. It:

  • Enumerates cards by I²C probe at boot: each card self-identifies through its on-card address jumpers (§5.2), so the controller simply probes the known address set (CH1 0x48/0x0C/0x70, CH2 0x49/0x0D/0x71, AFE 0x4A ADC + 0x0E offset-null DAC) and confirms each expected card ACKs. There are no slot straps to read.
  • Sets operating points via each card's on-board DAC (channel A = laser, B = TEC) with cached write-on-change, so an unchanged set-point generates zero bus traffic.
  • Reads telemetry (laser IMON, TEC IMON, NTC, PD levels) from the on-card ADS1115s at ≤10 Hz round-robin, and suspends I²C polling entirely while either channel is acquiring lock, so bus activity never folds into the PDH demodulator.
  • Handles faults on the shared open-drain FAULT_n line — edge-detect, query each card's GPIO expander to localise, park safe.
The full pin and I²C address map (CDCE913 0x65; per-channel ADS1115 / DAC / GPIO) is in config.h and TeensyFirmwareAssumptions.txt. Build under Teensyduino before flashing.
Address convention — resolved. The ADG419 mux truth table (IN = 0 → S1 = ERR1/MOD1, verified against the datasheet) fixes the hardware convention as channel 1 = GA0 = 0 (0x48/0x0C/0x70), i.e. JP101 fitted. config.h now matches, so the CH1/CH2 constant swap an earlier revision of this manual called for is no longer needed — do not apply it. GA readback pins 14/15/16 no longer carry slot straps.

15.8 ERR offset null (automatic)

The AD835 demodulator has an output offset of its own (±25 mV typical, ±75 mV worst case), and the local-oscillator drive leaks a little energy straight through to the output. Together these form a DC pedestal on ERRn that the ×5.02 output stage multiplies to as much as ±377 mV. Because the CH-card servo drives the laser until ERR equals its own datum, a pedestal is indistinguishable from a real error and simply displaces the lock point.

The pedestal drifts with temperature and with LO level, so the trimpots of the earlier revision needed periodic re-adjustment. They are gone, replaced by the AD5696R at 0x0E: firmware sweeps the DAC until the measured error sits on VREF_MID, then stores the code in EEPROM.

The routine first drives the DAC to both endpoints to confirm the pedestal actually lies inside the trim range. If it does not, it aborts and leaves the previous code untouched rather than silently converging on an endpoint — a failure that would otherwise surface much later as a bad lock. An out-of-range report points at LO drive level, an unblocked beam, or a wrong resistor value in the VB network.

The web dashboard shows the live code on each channel card as a position bar within the DAC span, with a tick at midscale. The bar turns amber when the code is within 10 % of either end — the early warning that the pedestal has nearly outrun the available trim.

Injection, not direct drive. The DAC feeds the VB nodes through 100 kΩ, so the passive divider still dominates the node. A DAC that is dead, absent, or never written leaves ERR at ≈2.48 V — half a volt from centre — instead of driving a rail into the CH servo. That is deliberate: the failure mode is a small fixed offset, not a runaway.

16 Installation & bring-up

  1. Mechanical. Fit the backplane into the subrack; install two card guides per slot. Isolate the backplane from the chassis with the nylon shoulder washers.
  2. Wire the transformers. Chassis-mount T1/T2; land their secondaries on the pm22V and pm6V input terminals respectively. Confirm each center tap is connected before power-on.
  3. Power cards first, no signal cards. Insert pm22V, pm6V, pm1p8V only. Apply mains and verify, unloaded: ±18 V bus, ±6 V_TEC, then on pm1p8V the ±15 V, ±5 V, 5.000 V ref, 3V3, 1V8, +5V_LASER. Confirm the ±15 V rails come up equal-and-opposite.
  4. Add the DIG card. Confirm the Teensy boots, the TFT lights, I²C pull-ups are present, and the 10 MHz/DDS clock is alive.
  5. Set the CH address jumper. On one CH card fit JP101 (laser channel 1); on the other leave JP101 open (channel 2). That is the only strap on the card. See the tables in §5.2.
  6. Add AFE, then one CH card at a time. Verify each CH card enumerates at the I²C addresses its jumper selects (ch1: 0x48/0x0C/0x70; ch2: 0x49/0x0D/0x71). With lasers still parked, confirm TEC servo reaches setpoint and NTC reads plausibly.
  7. Laser enable. Only after PGOOD/power-good and with fiber safely terminated, allow firmware to ramp the laser DAC from 0. Confirm IMON tracks command.
  8. Null the ERR offset. With modulation running but the beam blocked, run null1 and null2. Each reports the code it found and the residual; both should settle within a fraction of a millivolt. An out-of-range report means the pedestal exceeds the trim range — check LO drive and that the beam really is blocked before going further (§15.8). The codes are stored in EEPROM, so this is a once-per-build step rather than a routine one.
  9. Lock. Unblock the beam. Verify the AFE produces a bipolar discriminator (ERRn swinging either side of 2.5 V) as the laser is swept across a fringe, then close the PI lock.
Bench diagnostic reminders. A shorted bridge or AC-on-a-DC-pad does not show when probing each raw rail to ground (you just charge that rail's cap) — probe RAW+ to RAW− and diode-test the bridge legs with a transformer lead lifted. Asymmetric split rails almost always mean a floating center tap.

18 Measurement uncertainty budget

This section quantifies how well the instrument measures its intended quantity — the differential temperature ΔT = TB − TA, read out as the laser beat frequency Δν = S·ΔT. It follows the GUM approach (Type A statistical + Type B modelled contributors, combined in quadrature to a standard uncertainty uc). Because the measurement is ratiometric and material-defined over a τ ≈ 60 s window, the operative figure of merit is not long-term reproducibility but the within-window Allan deviation at τ ≈ 60 s, expressed in equivalent kelvin.

Carrier ν0 = c/λ = 193.4 THz  (λ = 1550 nm)  ·  line FWHM = ν0/Q = 1.93 GHz (Q = 105)
Sensitivity S = dν/dT ≈ 2 GHz/K  ⇒  1 nK ↔ 2 Hz of beat. Every contributor is kept in Hz with a kelvin twin (÷ S).

18.1 Why the ratio cancels the slow terms

Both rings sit on one die and are read through the same chain, so any error that is slow compared with the 60 s window and common to both arms subtracts out of the beat: timebase aging, common-mode thermal leakage (ε·ΔTcommon), strain/mount creep, and slow drift of the residual amplitude modulation (RAM). These are the largest raw contributors, yet they do not enter the result. What survives is only the fast, in-band content between ≈1/τwindow and the loop bandwidth.

18.2.1 How each value is derived

Every contributor is modeled as a single Allan power law and read out at the 60 s window:

σ(τ) = a · τμ  (a = the 1 s coefficient, Hz)  ·  slope μ: white-FM −½, flicker 0, random-walk +½
value at 60 s = a · 60μ  ·  kelvin twin = Hz ÷ S = Hz ÷ (2 GHz/K)  ⇒  nK = Hz ÷ 2

The 1 s coefficients are engineering allocations at this design stage. Only the detection term is semi-first-principles (readout noise ÷ discriminator slope); the rest are modeled from component specs (Type B) and become statistical (Type A) once the in-loop error PSD is measured. The Type column marks which is which.

  • Detection / discrimination noise (Type A · white-FM · μ = −½). Photodiode shot noise, TIA input noise and demodulator noise, divided by the discriminator slope (Hz of laser frequency per volt of error), give a flat frequency-noise density Sdet, so σ(τ) = Sdet/√τ. Densities: dither ≈150 Hz/√Hz (kHz lock-in, OPA380 TIA, slope taken over the ≈1 GHz half-width); RF ≈20 Hz/√Hz (wideband OPA814 TIA + AD835 multiplier → a steeper MHz-derivative slope and more demod gain). ⇒ 150/√60 = 19 Hz (9.7 nK) · 20/√60 = 2.6 Hz (1.3 nK). Validate against the measured in-loop error PSD. The RF front end's own current noise is now measured rather than assumed: 1.6 pA/√Hz total at the demodulation frequency (NEP ≈ 1.7 pW/√Hz), dominated by Rf Johnson noise; photodiode dark current contributes 0.0087 pA/√Hz and drops out of the budget entirely — see §3.4. Above ≈ 10 µA of photocurrent the term that actually matters is signal shot noise.
  • Residual laser FM above loop BW (Type A · flicker · μ = 0). The DFB's own frequency noise that the servo cannot suppress above its loop bandwidth; flat in Allan, so it does not average down (σ = a). Value = free-running FM × (1 − loop gain): the kHz dither loop leaves ≈50 Hz (25 nK); the faster analog-PI RF loop suppresses more → ≈6 Hz (3.0 nK). Set by the achieved loop bandwidth — read it from the closed-loop error transfer.
  • RIN → lock-point pulling (Type B · flicker · μ = 0). Laser relative-intensity noise multiplies any residual discriminator offset and pulls the apparent lock point. Dither ≈60 Hz (30 nK); RF ≈5 Hz (2.5 nK) — the phase-sensitive RF demod rejects amplitude fluctuations that the baseband dither lock passes. Estimated from the laser RIN spec × the offset; reducible by nulling the offset.
  • RAM in-band (flicker) — the decider (Type B · flicker · μ = 0). Residual amplitude modulation at Ω that survives the window — unwanted AM riding on the intended FM. It is a property of the current-modulated laser and the injection path, not the lock scheme, so both columns carry the same ≈20 Hz (10 nK) allocation. This is the one term that does not shrink with a better lock and it decides whether RF lands near 4 or 11 nK (see the RAM note below). The 20 Hz is a placeholder — measure the RAM spectrum to replace it.
  • Carrier-dither smear residual (Type A · white · μ = −½ · dither only). Specific to the kHz scheme: the dither carrier broadens the recovered line and the post-demod residual averages down as white noise, ≈50 Hz/√Hz → 50/√60 = 6.5 Hz (3.2 nK) at 60 s. The RF derivative lock demodulates coherently against a phase-set reference and has no equivalent term (—).
  • Timebase / counter short-term (Type B · white · μ = −½). The frequency counter's reference oscillator (TCXO/OCXO/GPSDO) short-term instability × the beat frequency. Negligible here: ≈1 Hz/√Hz (dither, modest reference) → 0.1 Hz (0.06 nK); ≈0.1 Hz/√Hz (RF, better reference) → <0.1 Hz. It enters as fractional instability × Δν, so a small Δν and a disciplined reference keep it tiny.
Cancelled terms (random-walk · μ = +½; slow and common, so removed by the ratio — §18.1). Each grows as a·√60 toward the window, which is why they are the largest raw terms yet contribute nothing to the result:
  • RAM slow drift — slow random-walk of the RAM; a ≈ 50 Hz → 50·√60 = 387 Hz (194 nK).
  • Thermal common-mode leakage — ε·ΔTcommon·S: a whole-die temperature excursion moves both rings together, the residual set by the same-die coefficient mismatch ε ≈ 10−4; a ≈ 30 Hz → 232 Hz (116 nK).
  • Strain / mount creep — differential mount/strain relaxation; a ≈ 20 Hz → 155 Hz (77 nK).
Without ratiometric cancellation these would dominate and push the beat past ~750 nK by 600 s (dashed curves, Fig 18.1).

Combined rows. The surviving (kept) contributors are independent, so they add in quadrature; the expanded uncertainty applies the k = 2 coverage factor:

uc = √(Σ σi²)   Dither: √(19² + 50² + 60² + 20² + 6.5² + 0.1²) ≈ 83 Hz (42 nK) ·  RF: √(2.6² + 6² + 5² + 20² ) ≈ 22 Hz (11 nK)

Note how the quadrature sum exposes the design story: the dither budget is spread across detection/FM/RIN, while the RF budget is a single term — the 20 Hz RAM allocation is 85 % of the RF variance, which is why the RAM-spectrum measurement decides the floor.

The RAM spectrum sets the RF floor. Measure the current-modulated laser's RAM spectrum early — it, not the electronics, is the likely limit, and every RF design choice (bias-tee injection, avoiding gate coupling) is biased to add as little RAM as possible.

19.2 What changes physically

19.2.1 The win — linewidth

The discriminant slope of any slope-detection lock goes as 1/FWHM. A 50 MHz π-FBG against the ring's 1.93 GHz line is a factor of 38 on every "fraction of a linewidth" error: detection noise, RIN pulling, and RAM lock offset all scale down together, provided the modulation depth is scaled down with the linewidth (§19.4.2). The carrier-smear residual goes to zero for the same reason — the depth needed on a 0.6 pm line no longer drags the carrier across an appreciable part of the resonance.

19.2.2 The loss — sensitivity

S falls from 2 GHz/K to 1.25 GHz/K, so the same frequency uncertainty is 1.6× more kelvin. This is why the FBG budget is kept in hertz and converted second, and why the two references must be compared in kelvin rather than in hertz.

19.2.4 Strain — the term that would kill it, and the topology that does not save it

At 150 MHz/µε the grating is a strain gauge that happens to respond to temperature: nanokelvin equivalence demands ~8 fε of strain stability, which no packaged fiber achieves open-loop. What makes the instrument work is that common strain is slow and largely common-mode, so the ratio removes it — leaving only the differential creep between the two grating mounts, which it does not.

The two gratings cannot share one fiber. It is tempting to write both gratings into one fiber between common anchors, so that axial strain is shared identically. That does not work here: §19.3.2 requires the pair to be matched to ±10 pm so the beat lands near 100 MHz, and two gratings that close in wavelength have almost completely overlapping stop bands — each laser would be reflected by the other grating and could never reach its own. The head must therefore use two separate fibers, one grating each, bonded to a common substrate across a thermal barrier. That is the direct analogue of two rings on one die, and it matches the AFE's two independent detection channels exactly.

Consequence for the budget: common-strain rejection is then a property of the mount, not of the glass. The coefficient-match figure ηε ≈ 10−3 used below is an assumption about bonding symmetry and must be measured on the assembled head, not taken from a datasheet. It is the least-defended number in this section.

19.3 The optical head

19.3.1 Layout

Two π-FBGs, one per fiber, bonded to a common substrate with a thermal barrier between them. Separation ≥ 15 mm (≈2× the 60 s thermal diffusion length): that spacing is what makes the differential measurement meaningful and what decorrelates the two gratings' thermodynamic noise, which is why that term carries the √2. Mount symmetrically — matched adhesive, matched bond length, common anchors — because differential creep is the strain term the ratio cannot remove.

19.3.2 Pair matching and the trim

Catalogue centre-wavelength tolerance is ±0.1 to ±0.2 nm, i.e. ±12 to ±25 GHz of Bragg offset, against a nominal beat of 100 MHz. Even a matched pair leaves a worst-case 2.5 GHz. The pair must be trimmed into beat range, and there are only two levers:

Make the strain trim passive. Having matched the pair, close the residual ~17 µε with a one-time mechanical stretch, locked down at assembly. Do not build an active PZT driver channel: an active trim injects its driver noise straight onto the measurand through Sε = 150 MHz/µε, and there is no HV rail in the crate (±22 V maximum) to drive one with. The absence of a PZT channel is therefore not a gap in the design.

19.3.3 How to buy a matched pair — do not pay for absolute tolerance

The instrument needs the two gratings close to each other. It does not care where they sit in absolute terms, because the DFBs tune to find them. That distinction is worth real money: a tight absolute centre-wavelength spec is a premium custom order, while a tight relative match can be had from loosely-binned stock simply by buying a batch and picking the closest two.

For N gratings scattered across a tolerance window W, the expected gap between the closest pair is ≈ W/N². Against O/E Land's ±1 nm stock grade at ~$45/pc, that gives:

19.4.3 Connectors — the one as-built choice that fights the FBG budget

§4.3 specifies FC/APC on the laser side and FC/PC at the detector. The laser-side APC choice is correct and matters more than ever. The detector-side PC endface does not survive the move to an FBG, and the reason is specific to gratings: a π-FBG is itself a mirror. Outside its narrow notch the grating reflects almost everything, so the grating and the detector endface form a real, strong etalon — a high-reflectivity mirror against a 3.5 % one — whose FSR is set by the FBG-to-detector fiber length. At ~1 m that is ~100 MHz, sitting directly on top of a 50 MHz grating linewidth, and its phase drifts with fiber temperature. This is the parasitic-etalon term of §19.2.3, and it is the second-largest surviving line in the budget.
There is no FC/APC version of the FGA01FC — do not go looking for one. Thorlabs supplies that photodiode with an FC/PC bulkhead only, and an APC ferrule must never be mated into a PC receptacle: the 8° endface tilts the beam off the Ø120 µm active area and the flat-to-angled contact leaves an air gap. Nothing has to be custom-built, but the detector is not a connector swap either. There are two supported routes.

The TIA does not care, and the pigtail need not be PM. The closed-loop bandwidth is f−3dB = 1/(2π·Rf·Cf) = 19.7 MHz — set by the feedback pair, not by the diode. A lower-capacitance detector drops CTOT from 5.50 pF to ≈4.07 pF and the flat-compensation value from 0.66 pF to 0.57 pF, so the fitted 1 pF stays comfortably above what stability requires; the margin improves. Check the reverse-voltage rating before ordering. The AFE biases its detectors from PD_BIAS_12V, and many telecom pigtailed PINs are specified at Vr = 5 V with a maximum well below 12 V. Either select a part rated ≥12 V or re-divide the bias to 5 V — at 5 V the junction capacitance is still far below the FGA01FC's 2.13 pF, so the TIA compensation above remains valid either way. This is the one substitution that can quietly destroy the detector.

19.4.4 Firmware, not hardware

Acquisition is the real work. The lock feature is 38× narrower: with the 16-bit laser DAC over 90.5 mA (1.38 µA/LSB) and DFB current tuning of ~2 GHz/mA, the ring's 1.93 GHz line spans ~690 DAC codes but a 50 MHz grating spans only ~18. Still catchable, but the sweep step, dwell, and lock-detect thresholds must all be retuned, and a full sweep takes proportionally longer. The omega1/omega2 commands stay inside the existing 100 kHz–3.5 MHz range, so no CLI change is needed.

What "PDH" means here. As with the ring (§3.1), this is not textbook Pound–Drever–Hall. True dispersive PDH on a 0.6 pm (75 MHz) line needs Ω ≳ 75 MHz of phase modulation, which neither laser-current FM nor the AD9833 on its 25 MHz clock can reach. The FBG is locked with the same 2–2.5 MHz derivative discriminator the ring uses. The linewidth gain is fully retained — it belongs to the discriminator slope, not to the modulation scheme. Reaching true PDH would require an EOM and driver, a >75 MHz LO, and a faster TIA (19.7 MHz is the binding limit); the AD835 would survive. That is an AFE respin, not a retrofit, and the budget below does not assume it.

19.4.5 π-shifted or standard — which fits this crate

§19.1 rejects the standard telecom FBG on the uncertainty number alone. The hardware argument is independent of that and reaches the same verdict more strongly, because the two classes stress different parts of the crate — and the standard grating stresses the part that cannot be fixed without a respin.

Modulation demand. Depth is specified as a fraction of the fringe half-width (§19.4.2), so the required injection current scales linearly with linewidth off the as-built 0.30 mA point. That scaling holds whatever the DFB's exact FM coefficient turns out to be:

19.5.4 Voltage twin

Used as a voltmeter the response is square-law, ν = k1V2 (§18.4). For a thermally-coupled transducer the full-scale beat swing scales with S, so the ring's 14 GHz over 2 V becomes 8.7 GHz over 2 V for the FBG, i.e. k1 = 2.18 GHz/V² and a slope at full scale of 8.7 GHz/V.

1 Hz → 114.5 pV = 5.72×10−5 µV/V   (ring: 71.4 pV — the FBG is 1.6× worse per hertz)
1 nK → 1.25 Hz → 143 pV = 7.14×10−5 µV/V   (ring: 143 pV — invariant)

The kelvin→volt conversion is the same for both references, because νFS and S scale together. Only the hertz→volt conversion differs. As in §18.4 there is no operating-point-independent figure of merit: absolute uncertainty grows as 1/V and relative as 1/V², so every voltage number above is quoted at VFS = 2 V and must be rescaled before being carried anywhere else.

19.6 Bill of design consequences

What adopting the FBG option commits you to, beyond the parts themselves:

  1. Buy π-phase-shifted gratings as a batch, not a pair — one draw, one phase mask, notch FWHM ≤0.6 pm (≈75 MHz), and have the vendor select the closest two for ≤10 pm relative match (§19.3.3). Absolute centre wavelength is uncritical and narrower notches buy nothing (§19.4.5); pay for the relative match instead.
  2. PM fiber throughout, aligned launch, specified PER. The non-PM row of §19.5.3 prices the alternative at 52 nK — worse than the FBG's own worst case.
  3. Two fibers, one grating each, on a common substrate, ≥15 mm apart across a thermal barrier (§19.2.4, §19.3.1).
  4. Passive strain trim, ~20 µε range, set once and locked (§19.3.2). No active PZT channel, no TEC trim.
  5. All-spliced optical path; FC/APC wherever a connector is unavoidable. Isolator after each DFB as before, plus a decision on the detector interface — isolator retrofit or pigtailed detector, since the FGA01FC has no APC variant (§19.4.3).
  6. Change Rinj from 2 kΩ to ≈51 kΩ per channel (§19.4.2).
  7. Retune acquisition firmware for an ~18-code lock feature (§19.4.4).
  8. Mount symmetrically and verify ηε — with two separate fibers this is a measured mounting property, not a datasheet number (§19.2.4).
Measure these three first, in this order: (1) the fiber thermal-noise floor of the actual grating pair, (2) the parasitic-fringe amplitude of the assembled optical path, (3) polarisation stability. Between them they move the result from ~5 nK to ~46 nK; nothing else in the budget has comparable leverage. This is the FBG's equivalent of "measure the RAM spectrum early" in §18.
